Holiday shopping scams in search results: a family safety guide

A familiar store name and a polished search listing can make an unfamiliar shopping site feel safe. The Federal Trade Commission’s November 19, 2026 consumer alert warns that scam sellers may pay to place ads near the top of holiday shopping results. A few calm checks can help families confirm where a link leads before anyone enters payment or personal information.

What the FTC alert says

Published on November 19, 2026, the FTC alert describes how scammers can use paid search ads that appear alongside legitimate listings, including near the top of results. A link may lead to a fake store that tries to take a shopper’s money or collect information such as a bank account number. The alert offers prevention guidance; it does not describe one specific victim, store, or dated criminal incident.

That distinction matters. A search result’s position is not a trust rating, and an ad appearing beside legitimate results does not establish that the seller has been vetted. This is a useful reminder for any shopper, especially when a familiar brand or a hard-to-find gift makes an offer feel time-sensitive.

A simple check-before-buying routine

1. Don’t treat the first listing as the official store

Pause before opening a top result. The FTC recommends scrolling past paid ads and looking at the regular results below them. If the shopper already knows the retailer’s official web address, typing it directly into the browser is another way to avoid a lookalike listing. A small “sponsored” label is easy to miss, so take a moment to read what kind of result is shown.

2. Check the seller independently

For a store or seller you don’t recognize, search the business name along with words such as “complaint” or “scam,” then compare what you find across sources. The FTC’s broader online shopping guidance also recommends reading seller and product reviews critically; star ratings alone can be misleading. These checks can surface concerns, but a lack of complaints does not prove a new seller is safe.

3. Look at the actual address before sharing details

Before entering a password, address, or payment details, check the website address in the browser bar. Look for misspellings, extra words, or a web address that does not fit the retailer you meant to visit. If anything seems off, close the page and start again from an address you know or a trusted source.

4. Review the purchase terms

Check the return, shipping, and refund details and save the order confirmation and receipt. Be cautious if the site pushes an unusually urgent deal or payment method that is hard to reverse. The FTC’s shopping advice explains what purchase records to keep and what to do if an order does not arrive.

How family members can help without taking over

Offer to look at the listing together and ask, “Would you like to check the seller with me before you order?” Let the shopper control the device and make the final decision. You can compare the address, search the store name independently, and read the return policy side by side. The aim is a repeatable habit, not a test of someone’s judgment.

If the shopper already entered card details or paid and now suspects a fake store, contact the card issuer or payment provider promptly and explain what happened. Ask whether the payment can be stopped or disputed. Save the listing, receipts, and messages, and report suspected fraud to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov. For an order that never arrives, the FTC’s recovery guidance includes steps for different payment types.

A useful family rule is simple: pause on an unfamiliar seller, verify it from a separate route, and only then decide whether to buy. That small pause can make online holiday shopping feel more manageable without turning every offer into a reason for alarm.

Sources and reporting notes: The FTC’s “How to avoid holiday shopping scams in your search results” was published November 19, 2026 and discusses search-ad shopping scams as a pattern, not a dated individual event. Seller checks and purchase-record advice draw on the FTC’s online shopping guide and recovery guide.
