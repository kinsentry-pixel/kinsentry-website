Fake FTC recovery scams: what families should know

After someone loses money to a scam, a message promising to get it back can feel like a lifeline. The FTC warned on June 3, 2026 that impostors may claim to be FTC “agents” who can recover money lost in a scam. A separate FTC alert published November 12, 2026 explains how fake FTC websites and other impersonators try to steal money or personal information. These alerts describe scam tactics, not one specific victim incident.

What the FTC alerts say

The FTC’s June alert says scammers impersonate FTC employees to get money, financial-account access, or personal information. In the recovery version, the scammer falsely claims to be an FTC “agent” who can help recover money lost in an earlier scam. The November alert adds that impostors may use fake badges, emails, texts, calls, social media messages, and websites that copy the FTC’s look. It says FTC websites end in .gov, including ReportFraud.ftc.gov.

The FTC says its employees will not tell people to transfer money or hand over cash to “protect” it. A badge image, official-sounding title, case reference, or convincing website does not verify someone’s identity. Scammers can copy logos and caller ID details.

How to respond to a recovery offer

  1. Pause before replying. Don’t pay an upfront fee, buy gift cards or cryptocurrency, share a one-time code, or give remote access to a device because someone promises recovery.
  2. Don’t use the contact details they provide. Open a fresh browser window and type ReportFraud.ftc.gov yourself. The FTC notes that real FTC sites use the .gov domain. If you need help, reach a known bank or payment provider using the number on your card or its official app.
  3. Keep the messages and payment records. Save emails, texts, phone numbers, receipts, wallet addresses, and screenshots. Don’t forward personal documents or account details to an unverified contact.
  4. Tell someone you trust. A family member can help check a website address or contact the bank, while keeping the person targeted involved in each decision.

Reporting a scam to the FTC helps the agency spot patterns and build cases, but a report is not a promise that money will be recovered. The FTC’s consumer guidance recommends contacting the company used to send money as soon as possible and asking whether a transfer can be stopped or reversed.

If personal information or money was shared

Contact the bank, card issuer, payment app, gift-card company, wire service, or cryptocurrency platform right away using a contact method you find independently. Explain that the payment was connected to a scam and ask what steps are available. Recovery is not guaranteed, but a prompt report may help the provider act.

If someone shared account passwords, change them from a trusted device and enable two-factor authentication. If they shared a Social Security number, the FTC directs people to IdentityTheft.gov for a recovery plan. Report the impersonation or other scam at ReportFraud.ftc.gov. If a person allowed remote access to a computer, follow the FTC’s steps for securing the device and accounts.

A supportive response matters. Try: “I’m glad you told me. Let’s contact the bank using the number we already trust, then report the messages.” A second scam attempt is the scammer’s responsibility; asking for help early is a sensible next step.

Sources and reporting notes: The FTC’s June 3, 2026 alert, “A real FTC employee won’t text you their photo ID to ‘verify’ their identity”, describes the false FTC-agent recovery offer. The FTC’s November 12, 2026 alert about fake FTC websites and impostors covers official domains and verification. Practical recovery steps are based on the FTC’s “What To Do if You Were Scammed” guidance. Dates refer to publication of the FTC guidance; the alerts do not describe one dated victim event. This article separates those confirmed points from family-oriented advice.
