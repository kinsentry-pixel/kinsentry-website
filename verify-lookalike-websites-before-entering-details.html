Before you click: how to check lookalike websites

A link to a rebate, patient-savings offer, sweepstakes, or settlement claim can feel routine—especially when it arrives in a printed notice or a familiar-looking ad. But a website address that differs by just one letter can lead somewhere else. A new security research report describes lookalike domains appearing shortly after real campaign addresses were registered. A simple family habit can help: pause, find the destination independently, and verify the address before entering personal details.

What the new research found

On November 16, 2026, Symantec's security research team published an analysis of an ongoing typosquatting campaign. Researchers documented one-letter lookalike domains associated with well-known businesses. The fake addresses were registered within the next working day of the genuine domains. The analysis said many legitimate addresses were intended for campaign pages such as patient-savings programs, sweepstakes, rebates, claims portals, and legal settlements.

Researchers reported that the lookalike sites served parked pages, deceptive search portals, or brand-themed blogs with ads that could route visitors to scam sites. They said the campaign was still being tracked, with new lookalikes registered as recently as November 11, 2026. That is the latest activity date cited in the report, not proof that every affected brand or visitor was harmed.

Date context: November 16 is the research publication date; November 11 is the latest registration activity the report describes. The report documents domains and site behavior. It does not claim that every lookalike page stole money or personal data.

Why a legitimate-looking link can still be risky

Some real offers ask people to visit a particular web address and provide information to check eligibility or submit a claim. A lookalike can exploit that expectation. One missing letter, an extra character, or a different ending such as “.com” instead of another domain ending may be easy to overlook on a phone screen.

The U.S. Department of the Treasury's November 18, 2026 holiday scam advisory names online sales fraud, phishing and spoofing, gift-card fraud, and charity scams among common seasonal threats. Its accompanying advisory also recommends independently contacting a person or institution through another channel when verifying a request. These are broad seasonal warnings; they do not identify the specific typosquatting campaign above.

A three-minute link check for families

1. Don’t start from the message

If an unexpected email, text, social post, or search ad asks you to claim a benefit or enter account details, don’t use its link as your only route. Find the organization’s official website from a saved bookmark, a previous statement, or a trusted source you already use. If it is a claim or settlement, check the paperwork and contact information independently.

2. Read the whole address

Before typing anything into a page, look at the domain in the browser’s address bar. Check the spelling carefully, including whether a word is singular or plural and what comes immediately before the first slash. A padlock or “https” indicates an encrypted connection; it does not prove that the site belongs to the company it names.

3. Ask a second person to confirm

For an unfamiliar benefits or payment page, take a moment to show the address to a family member or trusted friend. Use a phone number from the back of a card, an official statement, or the organization’s known website—not a number supplied in the suspicious message. The FTC's phishing guidance also recommends contacting a company using a phone number or website you know is real.

If information was already entered

Use the real organization’s website or a trusted phone number to change any password you submitted, and change it anywhere else you reused it. If you entered card or bank details, call the financial institution using the number on the card or statement and ask what protective steps to take. Save the message and the web address, then report suspected fraud to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov. Acting quickly can help limit further exposure, though it cannot guarantee that a payment or information can be recovered.

Families can make the routine feel supportive rather than corrective: “Let’s check this together before we submit it.” A second look is useful for anyone, and it gives a loved one space to make their own decision with better information.

Sources and dates: Symantec, “Typosquatters Copy Big Brands’ New Domains Within a Day of Registration,” published November 16, 2026; U.S. Department of the Treasury, holiday scam advisory release, November 18, 2026; Federal Trade Commission, phishing guidance.
