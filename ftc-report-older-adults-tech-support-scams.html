FTC report: older adults remain at higher risk from tech-support scams

A new Federal Trade Commission report offers a useful reminder for families: tech-support fraud still deserves attention. In the FTC's analysis of 2025 reports, adults 60 and older were more likely than younger adults to report losing money to tech-support scams. Here is what that finding does—and does not—say, and how to turn it into a simple family plan.

What the FTC's 2026 report found

The FTC's Protecting Older Consumers 2025–2026 report, published in October 2026, reviews consumer fraud reports submitted for 2025. After adjusting for population size, adults 60 and older were 324% more likely than adults 18–59 to report losing money to a tech-support scam.

That comparison needs context. It describes the relative rate of reports that involved a loss; it does not mean that three in four older adults were scammed, nor does it measure every scam that happened. The FTC also recorded about $126 million in reported tech-support scam losses among older adults in 2025, 21% lower than the year before. Those figures can both be true: total reported losses fell, while older adults remained more likely than younger adults to report losing money in this category.

The report also says older adults were more likely to report losing money to scams impersonating friends or family. Fake computer warnings and urgent calls can therefore fit into a wider pattern: a stranger creates a problem, claims to be a trusted helper, and pushes someone to act before they verify the story.

How a fake computer alert turns into a money scam

The FBI describes a familiar sequence: a pop-up, text, call, or search result claims there is a problem with a computer or account. The person is told to call a number, hurry, and let a supposed support agent connect remotely. Once they have access, scammers may look for personal information or steer the victim toward a payment such as a wire, gift card, or cryptocurrency transfer.

A real security warning may be inconvenient, but it does not require you to call a number displayed in a browser window or hand control of your computer to an unsolicited caller. Close the page if you can. If it will not close, use the browser or computer's normal quit or restart controls, then contact the company through a number or website you already trust.

A three-step family response

1. Pause before calling or clicking

Agree on one rule with your parent: an unexpected pop-up or caller never gets an immediate response. Do not call the number on the alert, install software, share a one-time code, or let a stranger connect remotely.

2. Verify through a separate route

Close the message and find the company's contact details independently, such as on a bill, the back of a bank card, or its official website typed into a fresh browser tab. If the caller claims to be a relative, call that person or another family member using a saved number.

3. If someone already connected, act quickly

End the remote session and disconnect the computer from the internet if you need to stop an active connection. From a different, trusted device, contact the bank or payment provider, change passwords that may have been exposed, and enable multifactor authentication. Preserve messages and receipts. The FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center accepts reports about tech-support fraud; the FTC also accepts reports at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.

Make the plan easy to remember

A short family phrase can help: “Stop, close it, call me.” It gives someone a next step when a screen is frightening or a caller sounds convincing. Practice the rule before an emergency, and make sure your parent knows they can ask for help without being blamed for clicking or answering.

KinSentry can add a layer of protection against known scam pages and suspicious pop-ups, but family verification still matters. The strongest routine combines device safeguards with a trusted person to call before money, passwords, or remote access are involved.
