Fake Microsoft pop-ups: what families should do

A full-screen “virus” warning can make it hard to think clearly, especially when it flashes, sounds an alarm, or says not to turn off the computer. An alert published by Australia’s Scamwatch on November 20, 2026 describes scammers using fake Microsoft and Windows pop-ups to persuade people to call bogus support numbers. The alert describes a current scam pattern, not one specific dated victim incident.

What the November alert confirms

Scamwatch says the fake warnings may take over a screen, play alarms or recorded voices, and claim a device is hacked or infected. The page may show a phone number and tell the person not to turn off the computer. If someone calls, the person answering may pretend to be Microsoft support and ask them to install remote-access or screen-sharing software. That access can expose personal information and online banking or enable unauthorized transactions.

The alert says these pages can appear after links in ads, fake news stories, social-media notifications, free online games, and paid search results. It also notes that anyone can be targeted. The goal is to create urgency so a person acts before checking the story. The FTC likewise advises that a real security pop-up will not ask someone to call a phone number.

A calm response when a warning takes over

  1. Do not call the number or install anything. Don’t share passwords, banking details, or verification codes. Don’t allow a caller to connect remotely, even if they claim to be Microsoft or another familiar company.
  2. Close the browser safely. On Windows, Scamwatch lists Alt + F4 to close the active browser window, or Ctrl + Shift + Escape to open Task Manager and end the browser task. On a Mac, it lists Command + Option + Escape to open Force Quit and quit the browser. Ending a task may discard unsaved work. If unsure, ask a trusted person for help. Don’t follow instructions from the pop-up itself.
  3. Verify separately. If you still have a concern about the device, contact the manufacturer or a trusted technician using contact information you find independently. Update security software and run a scan. Never use a number or link supplied by the alarming page.
  4. Review browser notifications. If the messages return, remove unfamiliar sites from the browser’s notification permissions and clear its cookies and cache. Scamwatch provides these steps as follow-up measures for this pop-up pattern.

For an older family member, a calm offer can help: “Let’s close this page and check with someone we already trust.” The person targeted should stay in control of their device when possible. The point is to give everyone a familiar pause-and-verify routine, not to make anyone feel blamed for seeing a convincing warning.

If someone already called or shared access

End the call and disconnect the device from the internet. Contact the bank or card provider immediately if account details, payment information, or money may be exposed. From a separate, trusted device, change passwords for accounts accessed during the session, starting with email and banking, and turn on multifactor authentication where available. Use reputable security software to scan the device, or ask a trusted technician to check it before using it for sensitive accounts again.

Save the phone number, messages, payment records, and a description of what happened. Report the incident to the relevant consumer-protection or cybercrime agency in your country. In the United States, the FBI’s Internet Crime Complaint Center accepts reports about tech-support scams, and the FTC accepts fraud reports. If money was sent, ask the bank or payment provider promptly whether a transfer can be stopped or a charge disputed.

A browser warning can look official and still be a webpage designed to frighten the person viewing it. Close it, verify through a separate route, and bring in someone trusted before taking any action.

Sources and reporting notes: Australia’s Scamwatch published “Scam alert: Microsoft pop-up IT support scams” on November 20, 2026. It describes a pattern and gives browser-closing and safety guidance; it does not report one dated victim event. General advice is also informed by the FTC’s tech-support scam guidance and the FBI’s tech-support scam guidance. The article distinguishes details confirmed in those sources from practical family advice and is an original explainer.
