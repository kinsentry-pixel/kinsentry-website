Why older adults are 5× more likely to lose money to tech-support scams

It's tempting to assume that people who fall for scams are naive or careless. The data tells a different story. Older adults are five times more likely than younger people to report losing money to tech-support scams — and it has almost nothing to do with intelligence. It has everything to do with how these scams are designed.

That gap isn't an accident. Scammers don't stumble onto older adults — they choose them, and engineer every step of the approach around a handful of predictable human pressures. Understanding those pressures is the first step to protecting the people you love.

This isn't a story about gullible victims. It's a story about professional manipulation aimed at a carefully chosen target.

The three levers scammers pull

  1. Authority

    Many older adults grew up in an era when institutions — the phone company, the bank, "the computer people" — were trusted by default. A caller who sounds official and says "I'm from Microsoft" or "this is your bank's fraud department" carries real weight. Scammers borrow that authority to lower a person's guard before they've said anything suspicious.

  2. Urgency

    Almost every one of these scams manufactures a crisis: "Your account is being drained right now." "Do not turn off your computer." "You have twenty minutes." Panic is the point — under acute stress, careful people stop thinking clearly and start following instructions. This works on anyone; scammers simply reach for it early and hard.

  3. Isolation

    Many older adults spend more time alone, with no one nearby to glance over and say, "That sounds like a scam — hang up." Worse, a friendly scammer can fill that gap, presenting themselves as the helpful, patient voice on the other end. The absence of a second opinion is one of the most powerful advantages a scammer has.

The technology gap — not a knowledge gap

It's easy to say older adults are "bad with computers," but that misses the point. The real issue is that the line between a real system alert and a convincing fake one is genuinely hard to see if you didn't grow up with these systems. A well-made scam pop-up looks identical to a legitimate warning. Expecting someone to spot the difference in a moment of panic isn't reasonable — for anyone.

What research suggests about trust and age

Studies of aging and decision-making point to a few tendencies that make later life more pleasant but can be exploited: older adults often focus more on positive information and may be less attuned to the subtle cues that signal deception. In other words, the same warmth and trust that make someone a wonderful parent or grandparent are exactly what a scammer is counting on. That's not a flaw to be ashamed of — it's a human trait being weaponized.

💙

Blame backfires. Shame makes victims hide what happened and delay getting help — which is exactly what the scammer wants. These operations fool professionals and younger people too. The productive response is practical, not judgmental.

What actually lowers the risk

You can't out-argue a scammer's psychology, but you can take away the conditions they depend on:

  • Talk about it before it happens

    Walk through how these scams work, calmly, when there's no pressure. A person who's heard "scammers pretend to be Microsoft" is far harder to catch off guard.

  • Agree the one rule that matters

    "If anyone ever asks you to install software or read them a code — hang up and call me first." It defeats the urgency and the isolation in a single sentence.

  • Reduce the isolation

    Regular contact means there's always someone to sanity-check a strange call — and someone the scammer has to compete with.

  • Put a safety net on the browser

    Because you can't be there for every call, protection that blocks the scam sites and alerts you closes the gap the rest of the time.
