How to protect your elderly parents from online scams

If you're worried that a scammer could talk your mum or dad out of their savings, you're not being paranoid — you're paying attention. Scams aimed at older adults are more convincing and more common than ever, and telling a parent to "just be careful" doesn't work against a professional who does this all day. This guide walks through exactly how these scams work, and the practical, doable steps that actually reduce the risk — most of which take a single afternoon.

Why antivirus isn't enough

Most families assume that up-to-date antivirus keeps a parent safe. It doesn't — and understanding why is the single most important thing in this guide. Antivirus is built to block malware: hostile code that sneaks onto a computer. But the scams that empty older adults' accounts don't use malware at all.

Instead, they work by convincing the person to do the dangerous thing themselves — install real software, grant remote access, read out a code, move money. From the computer's point of view, nothing is wrong; a real person is simply using it normally. The breach is human, not technical, which is exactly why security software waves it straight through.

You can't patch trust. That's why protecting a parent is about the process, not just the software on the machine.

The 3 scams that target seniors most

1. Remote-access & tech-support scams

A fake "virus warning" pop-up or an unsolicited call convinces your parent to install a remote-access tool (like AnyDesk or TeamViewer) and hand over control of their computer. The scammer then performs a fake "diagnosis" and directs payment. This is the most damaging category — and the one KinSentry is built for. For a full step-by-step breakdown, read the anatomy of a tech-support scam, and see how the scam pop-up itself gets blocked.

2. Phishing emails and fake alerts

Emails that look like they're from a bank, Amazon, or a delivery company, warning of a "problem" and linking to a fake login page. One click and the scammer has a password — or a foothold to start a phone call. Older adults are targeted heavily because a single convincing message can open the door.

3. Impersonation and "your account is compromised" calls

A caller claiming to be the bank's fraud department, a government agency, or a family member in trouble. The script is built on authority and urgency: act now, don't tell anyone, move the money to "keep it safe." No technology is even required — just a phone and a convincing story.

How to spot the warning signs

Scams rarely happen in a single instant; the signs are usually there for days. Watch for secretive calls with a "helper," new remote-access apps, unusual money movements, sudden urgency about an account, and mentions of a pop-up or a number they called. The 20-minute scam-proofing checklist

You don't need to be technical to make a real difference. Most of the protective value comes from a handful of simple steps you can do in one sitting:

  1. Have the "call me first" conversation

    Agree one rule, out loud: "If anyone ever asks you to install software, read them a code, or move money — hang up and call me first." This single sentence defeats the urgency and secrecy most scams depend on.

  2. Remove remote-access apps they don't use

    Check for AnyDesk, TeamViewer, or similar tools and uninstall any they don't actually need. If there's nothing to hand over, the most damaging scam can't complete.

  3. Turn on pop-up blocking and update the browser

    Make sure their browser is current and pop-ups are blocked, so fewer fake "virus alerts" ever reach them.

  4. Set a "never" list for the phone

    Agree that they will never buy gift cards for anyone who calls, never give remote access to an unsolicited caller, and never keep a "problem" secret from you. Legitimate companies never ask for these things.

  5. Turn on bank alerts

    Ask their bank to enable transaction alerts and, if available, a trusted-contact or large-transfer confirmation. Early notice of unusual activity buys precious time.

  6. Put a safety net on their browser

    Because you can't be there for every call, install protection that blocks the scam sites automatically and tells you the moment something is attempted.

Frequently asked questions

How do I know if my parent is being scammed?

Watch for secretive phone calls with a "technician" or "the bank," new remote-access apps like AnyDesk or TeamViewer, unusual money movements (gift cards, wires, withdrawals), sudden urgency about an account, and mentions of a pop-up warning or a number they called. Two or more of these together should be treated as an active scam.

What should I do if my parent already gave someone remote access?

Disconnect the computer from the internet, uninstall the remote-access app (AnyDesk, TeamViewer, etc.), and change important passwords from a different device. If any money moved, call the bank's fraud line immediately, then report it to the FTC and the FBI's IC3.

Is monitoring my parent's computer an invasion of privacy?

It depends entirely on the tool. Good scam protection is not surveillance: it should run on the device, never record the screen or browsing history, and only alert the family when an actual scam is blocked — never report on ordinary, everyday activity.

Does antivirus stop tech-support scams?

No. Tech-support scams contain no malware. They succeed by persuading the person to install real software and grant access themselves, which antivirus treats as legitimate. Protection built specifically for scams is what's needed.
